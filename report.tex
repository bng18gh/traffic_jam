\begin{abstract}
This report details the implementation of a control barrier function quadratic program (CBF-QP) algorithm as it relates to traffic management for merging autonomous vehicles. When vehicles are merging into a new lane, they must make sure that their surroundings make it safe to move over. The CBF-QP algorithm does this by evaluating a safety window around the vehicle before merging. Moreover, it then senses vehicles ahead of it in its network and can begin following them post merge. The results in this report show that when compared to a baseline right of way determining algorithm, the CBF-QP can guarantee merging behavior within a selected safety distance window while also completing the task faster on average.
\end{abstract}

autonomous vehicles, merging, control barrier function
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Motivation}
Traffic management is a key issue in autonomous vehicle driving. Every vehicle runs on its own planned trajectory based on global path planner algorithms, while also implementing obstacle avoidance through local path planning. However, oftentimes the obstacles in question are other vehicles, who are each also running through the same planning and obstacle avoidance. Interaction with other vehicles for safe traffic management is a key part of human driving, such as at freeway ramps or intersections, where cars will need to move from one place to another and ultimately coincide or collide with the path of another vehicle if nothing is done. The goal of this project is to implement a flocking control algorithm based on the implementations seen in [1]. This algorithm allows vehicles to act in a networked manner and is an example of the results of vehicle to vehicle communication. By maintaining awareness of the other vehicles as well as parameters such as speed, trajectory, and collision distance, this algorithm attempts to allow the vehicles to continue moving towards their desired destination with proper obstacle interaction with minimal time delay, avoiding collisions and staying safe.

\section{Simulation Environment}
The Matlab Autonomous Driving Toolbox is used to create a classic interaction environment for these vehicles – a lane merge. Merging lanes requires the key metrics desired from the motivation section, including vehicle merging trajectories, maintaining an ideal speed, and preventing collision with vehicles on the same path. 

In the visual examples provided in the GitHub repository, the network sets up a three lane road environment where one of the lanes is ending, and places a vehicle in each lane. The goal of the vehicles is to maintain their path forward without colliding with any other vehicles with as little interaction as needed to keep the algorithm light. What this essentially means is that the vehicle not in the disappearing lane or the merging lane will not need to be interacted with and it can continue on its path. 

Each car will self display its speed and merging state behavior, as well as other specific metrics based on the applicable control algorithm. The visual examples present in this code portfolio are used to demonstrate the effects of the control algorithms visually, and a separate script will handle the numerical data calculation.  


\section{Methodology and Implementation}
This paper evaluates the performance of two algorithms, which are being called the “baseline” algorithm and the control barrier function quadratic program “CBF-QP” algorithm. Each of these algorithms will complete the lane merging task at the most basic level, but the difference is how they choose to accomplish it. 

\subsection{Baseline Algorithm}\label{AA}
The baseline algorithm implements a right of way and yield based system, treating each car as a separate object rather than a combined network. Evaluation of merge availability begins at the control line, placed at a distance before the lane disappears to allow time for a safe merge. At the control line, each vehicle is evaluated for priority based on its right of way rules. When the merging lanes are side by side as in the visual example, the right of way goes to the vehicle that arrives at the control line first, or the furthest left lane if arrival times are the same. If the merging lanes are not side by side, the leftmost lane is always given priority. The other vehicle is then chosen to yield, which slows it down. 

Once yield behavior has been decided, the yielding vehicle continuously checks the merge lane to see if it is safe to merge into the new lane. This is done by evaluating whether the vehicle is further away than the safety horizon dictated in the simulation parameters. While the vehicle remains unsafe to merge, it stays in its lane and slows down to the unsafe yield speed marked in the parameters to extend the size of the gap until it is safe to merge. Figure 1 shows the vehicles during this yield behavior, during which the merging yellow car is deliberately slowing down to extend the gap between it and the orange car.

\subsection{CBF-QP Algorithm}
The CBF algorithm does not use a pure logic decision to assign merging behavior for vehicles. Rather, it evaluates safety in real time based on a distance boundary. Once again, evaluation begins at the control line. At the point of the control lane, the vehicle that needs to merge evaluates its position relative to the vehicle in the lane it needs to merge into. This boundary is circular, so it can evaluate whether the car in the merging lane is in front of or behind it. 

If the merging vehicle does not sense a vehicle within its boundary in the merging lane, it can begin its merging process. However, if it is not the vehicle will reduce its speed to let the car in the merging lane pass it, which will eventually allow for the boundary to be opened up and let the car merge as intended. The control barrier function helps apply new speeds to the vehicle that, unlike the baseline example, are not hard coded. All these speeds are still based on the collision boundary dictated earlier. However, this only needs to apply to the merging vehicle. The other vehicles can continue on their path unopposed, since they are not moving lanes. The function will only be necessary if a vehicle is merging into the lane or two vehicles are present in the same lane. Figure 3 shows an example of this merging behavior with the yellow car, indicated by its MERGE state. It is able to properly merge because no vehicle is present in its circle boundary, marked in dashed black. Additionally, the simulation does not return a crash from the orange car’s boundary overlapping with the blue car’s position, as they do not share a lane and neither vehicle is merging. Therefore, they do not interact per this algorithm. This is an improvement from the baseline algorithm, as the right of way decision making required that these vehicles interact at the control line and ultimately dropped the speed of the orange car, reducing the speed of the yellow car in turn.

Once the vehicle has successfully merged into its new lane, it changes its status to NO-MERGE. Additionally, the cohesion behavior of this algorithm begins its work. When two vehicles are in the same lane, the rear vehicle increases its speed to catch the vehicle in front of it until it reaches the boundary circle acting as the collision preventer. Figure 4 shows this catchup behavior, where the yellow car’s nominal speed is set at higher than the orange car’s nominal speed in an effort to close the gap between them.

\section{Conclusion}
Ultimately, some simple test cases and evaluations show the power of using the CBF-QP algorithm as a method of handling traffic merges for autonomous vehicles. This process treats the set of observed vehicles as a combined network rather than unique bodies, and thus each vehicle is able to sense other’s actions in its network, avoid collisions with their paths, and realign themselves with their partners to engage in following behavior. Moreover, it does not need a hierarchical right of way based system in order to do this – each vehicle simply senses its safety relative to vehicles around it while performing its actions. This ultimately results in a merging vehicle that avoids making close contact with neighboring vehicles when merging and accomplishes the merging behavior faster, allowing for larger networks to complete more merges with the same rules. The application of this algorithm in the future of autonomous vehicles will allow for vehicles that do not slow down and evaluate which car should move ahead like human drivers do. Rather, they will use their knowledge of the surroundings to seamlessly move into traffic and continue on their way, cleaning up the clutter and delay seen at traffic junctions worldwide.
